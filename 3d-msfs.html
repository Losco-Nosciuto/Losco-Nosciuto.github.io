From Chernobyl to the simulator

MSFS 2020, a flight over Chernobyl. The Duga antenna was not there. I had never opened Blender, but I wanted that radar on the ground more than I wanted to learn a new program. The first months were trial and error — tutorials, notes, and Blender Guru's Donut, which has nothing to do with game-ready assets but teaches you how the program thinks.

My only 3D background came from the late Nineties, Unreal Editor for Unreal and Unreal Tournament: no UV unwrapping, one texture per polygon or group of polygons. I started like that — many single textures, some in 2K, no trim sheets, no unwraps — and released two chaotic but working freeware mods for the two airfields closest to home: Chavez-Marini in Masera and Il Picchio in Borgo Ticino.

Then came unwrapping, one tutorial at a time, and texturing: first Quixel Mixer, then Adobe 3D Painter, reluctantly and with a fair amount of headaches. Painter is powerful and extremely articulated; it does not let you shout "user friendly". But it was the right deal.

After that, the collaboration with Microsoft and the partnership for MSFS, then MSFS 2024. Three payware airports — RK1 Kavik River Camp, EGLK BlackBushe, LFBP Pau Pyrénées — and two projects I never finished in time, because of the drive I formatted by mistake. There was a period of burnout, a sense of defeat. Then I went back to VCO VFR, more motivated than before.

Six steps, always in the same order

The sequence matters more than skill in any single step: skipping the scale or the LODs costs you in the simulator, with interest.

  1. 1

    Reference and blackboard

    PureRef, photos, charts, satellite data and in-sim screenshots. Everything on one board before opening Blender.

  2. 2

    Blockout and scale

    Real volumes, correct axes and pivots. If the scale is wrong, no texture saves it.

  3. 3

    Modeling and UV

    Blender, UV Packmaster 4 and the in-house suite: texture density, clean UVs, no wasted space.

  4. 4

    Texturing

    Substance 3D Painter and Sampler. A custom Python plugin brings images straight from the web into the project; node spaghetti is kept to a minimum.

  5. 5

    Animation

    Mixamo for about 80% of character animations; Cascadeur for the fixes and the rare handmade ones, where immersion needs it.

  6. 6

    Export, LOD and test

    MSFS 2024 SDK, glTF and LN LODs Pro for the LOD debug in Blender. Then in-sim tests at different distances, times and weather.

Immersion, in Simulation

Immersion is the keyword. A technically correct asset is not enough: scale, wear, light and animation have to convince the eye before the details do. The goal is not to show the model; it is to make the place feel real.

That is the standard I hold at LNDesign, from a freeware airfield to a payware airport: the same care on runway markings, fences, the dirt where vehicles turn, the way a character stands. If the user notices the asset, I have already lost.

LNDesign is where this standard lives, from freeware to payware.
