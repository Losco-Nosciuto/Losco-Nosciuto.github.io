PC Windows & IT, dual boot

Two systems, one workstation. Windows 11 for MSFS 2024 and Adobe; Nobara Linux 44 for daily work, rendering and development.

On this page

Background Setup What / Where / Why Lessons

Windows as a starting point, Linux as the evolution

Years on Windows taught the method: clean builds, driver discipline, and the habit of documenting every change. It also exposed the limits: a closed stack, background services you don't need, and a system that can change under your feet.

The move to Linux was practical, not ideological: a predictable environment, native tooling, and a desktop that doesn't move unless I move it. Nobara 44 is the daily driver because it is tuned for people who also work with graphics and games, without rebuilding the system by hand.

The workstation is a deliberate dual boot. Windows keeps MSFS 2024 and the Adobe suite; Nobara covers daily work, rendering, development and gaming. Each system does its job, and neither is bent to imitate the other.

What, where and why

The right tool in the right environment. A dual boot is not a compromise; it is a deliberate split.

Windows 11

What

MSFS 2024 + SDK · Substance 3D Painter / Sampler · Premiere Pro · After Effects · VS Code · Blender (MSFS assets, occasional)

Why

A single Adobe tool makes Windows mandatory, and the MSFS 2024 SDK and toolchain are Windows-only; Proton adds friction and unsupported edges. Blender itself is better on Linux, but the surrounding pipeline is not.

Nobara Linux

What

DaVinci Resolve Studio · Blender (rendering and non-game assets) · 2D graphics · Gaming · VS Code · Linux builds

Why

Daily driver and leisure: predictable updates, full control, open source where possible. Resolve Studio needs care but is excellent once configured; Linux builds and Flatpak are straightforward; gaming via Proton is smooth, with occasional compromises.

A design choice

Windows and Linux do different jobs. The workstation is arranged so neither has to imitate the other: no hacks, no half-working toolchains.

What I learned

  1. 1

    The backup comes first

    I lost years of work to a format: years of music the first time, years of work the second. My mistake, paid in full. Since then I am the most careful person I know on the subject: a verified copy of the projects, a second copy off the machine, and only then the format. It has nothing to do with the operating system: a wrong click, an update, a failing disk are enough. A backup that has never been restored is only a hope.

  2. 2

    Know the difference

    On Linux, kernel and drivers move together: one channel, one update, one rollback. On Windows they don't, and a blind update can leave a driver half-installed. Before updating I check the reports — forums, vendor notes, other people's tests — for incompatibilities with the software I work with, graphical glitches, thermal throttling. Ten minutes of reading is the difference between an update and a day of troubleshooting.

  3. 3

    Build your own tools

    In 2026 writing a small tool is cheap. On the other hand, opening the simulator, loading the project, compiling, testing, closing, changing one value, and starting again: that loop eats hours. Five hours on a helper script pay back in a week; a week on a tool that holds up pays back for years. Automate the passage you repeat, not the one you enjoy.

  4. 4

    The other system is not a guest

    This is not a Windows machine with Linux installed: it is two systems on one workstation, each with its own disk, its own boot and its own job. Moving daily tasks to Linux is the best technical decision I have made: predictable updates, native tools, full control. Windows stays where it is indispensable.

  5. 5

    Measure, don't guess

    Export times, temperatures, VRAM: the numbers say whether the bottleneck is the hardware, the driver or the project. A profile before and after every change turns opinions into evidence.
